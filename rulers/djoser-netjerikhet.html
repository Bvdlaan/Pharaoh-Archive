Old Kingdom

Djoser (Netjerikhet)

3rd Dynasty · 2686-2613 BCE

Reign begins2667 BCE
Reign ends2648 BCE
Dynasty span2686-2613 BCE

Background

Djoser, whose Horus name was Netjerikhet, is the best-attested ruler of Dynasty 3. His Step Pyramid complex at Saqqara is Egypt’s first major stone monument and transformed earlier royal funerary architecture into an enormous enclosed ceremonial landscape. The complex is traditionally associated with the official Imhotep, although the precise extent of Imhotep’s role is debated. Djoser’s monuments show the early Old Kingdom’s capacity to mobilise stone, labour and administration. His place in the dynasty is secure, even though absolute dates and some family relationships remain debated. Research: Metropolitan Museum; UCL Digital Egypt.

Chronology: Shaw, Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000)

Full profile

Restoring stability

Djoser is universally recognized as the founder of the Old Kingdom. Following the sectarian conflicts and northern revolts that defined the Second Dynasty, Djoser brought immediate stability to Egypt. This unified control over the northern delta region allowed him to launch state-sponsored mining expeditions into the Sinai Peninsula to extract valuable metals and turquoise.

The first stone pyramid

Djoser abandoned the ancestral royal cemetery of Abydos and relocated his mortuary complex to Saqqara, just outside the capital of Memphis. Here his chief architect and vizier, Imhotep, designed the Step Pyramid. Standing nearly 63 meters tall and consisting of six stacked mastabas, it was the first large-scale stone structure in human history. Underneath the pyramid, archaeologists discovered burial chambers for the king and eleven members of his family.

A lasting legacy

The Turin Canon assigns Djoser a relatively short reign of 19 years, yet his legacy endured for millennia. He was one of the few ancient pharaohs whose memory remained highly revered by later generations. When the scribe of the Turin Canon recorded Djoser's name, he broke convention by writing it in red ink instead of black to signify the king's profound historical importance.
