Old Kingdom

Sneferu (Snefru)

4th Dynasty · 2613-2494 BCE

Reign begins2613 BCE
Reign ends2589 BCE
Dynasty span2613-2494 BCE

Background

Sneferu founded Dynasty 4 and presided over a decisive transformation in pyramid architecture. His monuments at Meidum and Dahshur include the Bent Pyramid and the Red Pyramid; together they mark the successful development of the true smooth-sided pyramid. He is also attested in later records and royal cults, and the Palermo Stone records an expedition bringing cedar from Lebanon. The scale of his construction shows an increasingly powerful state, although the exact sequence and attribution of every project remain discussed. Sneferu’s reign laid the architectural and administrative foundations for the Giza pyramids. Research: Metropolitan Museum; UCL Digital Egypt.

Chronology: Shaw, Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000)

Full profile

A new dynasty

When King Huni passed away, he did not leave a male heir from his primary queen. Sneferu, the son of a minor wife who was also married to one of Huni's daughters (Hetepheres), ascended to the throne [00:02:27]. Because his mother belonged to a different branch of the royal family, ancient chroniclers placed Sneferu at the beginning of a new line of kings: the Fourth Dynasty [00:02:49].

The great pyramid builder

Sneferu inherited the previous dynasty's ambition for monumental architecture [00:03:50]. He commissioned or restored at least two, and possibly four, pyramids. He completed the pyramid at Meidum (likely started by Huni) and constructed both the Bent Pyramid and the Red Pyramid at Dahshur [00:04:14]. He is also associated with a smaller pyramid at Seila [00:04:22]. Historians now agree that these massive projects were not built by slaves, but by Egyptian citizens for whom serving the king was a profound honor [00:04:38].

Military campaigns and trade

Later generations remembered Sneferu as a benevolent ruler and a great warrior [00:04:58]. The Palermo Stone records his military campaigns to the west against Libyan forces and to the south into Nubia, returning with 7,000 prisoners and 200,000 cattle [00:05:14]. He also expanded trade, bringing back 40 ships filled with cedar wood from the Levantine city of Byblos [00:05:38]. In the Sinai Peninsula, rock carvings depict him smiting captives to assert control over the region's valuable copper and turquoise mines [00:06:02]. After a reign of roughly 24 years, he was likely buried in the Red Pyramid at Dahshur, though his body and sarcophagus have never been found [00:06:28].
